The New Operating Layer

AI has evolved well beyond discrete tools. It now functions as a continuous operating layer across media operations — reshaping how broadcasters, streamers, and content owners manage the full production and distribution chain.

To understand its impact, it helps to distinguish between three modes of AI deployment:

  • Automation: Rule-based systems executing configured tasks reliably at scale — transcoding pipelines, metadata tagging, scheduled publishing.
  • Orchestration: Coordinating multiple automated systems into connected workflows across teams and platforms.
  • Agents: Systems making contextual decisions within defined parameters, adapting to real-time data without explicit human instruction.

Each layer builds on the previous one. The organisations seeing the most value are those that have moved beyond point-solution automation and are beginning to connect capabilities at the orchestration and agent levels.

Building from the Ground Up

Most organisations don't begin with a grand AI strategy. They start by addressing specific friction points — archive retrieval backlogs, QC errors slipping through review, dubbing queues that slow international rollouts, or churn signals that arrive too late to act on.

As these individual capabilities mature and connect across newsrooms, production, advertising, and distribution teams, significant compound benefits emerge. Sky Italia's delivery platform demonstrates this at scale: using AI to route video data intelligently across infrastructure, maintaining buffer-free 4K streams while materially reducing operational costs.

"The organisations seeing the most value are those connecting AI capabilities across teams — not deploying isolated tools within a single department."

The Operational Reality

Three areas show the clearest transformation in day-to-day operations:

  • Operations teams gain faster anomaly detection and automated root cause analysis, reducing mean time to resolution during live events.
  • Localization processes handle translation, subtitling, and dubbing at a scale and speed that was previously impossible without proportional headcount growth.
  • Sports broadcasting benefits from real-time highlight generation, audience forecasting, and dynamic packaging — turning raw footage into monetisable assets within minutes of the final whistle.

The Problem of Trust

As AI-generated content proliferates, authenticity becomes a strategic concern, not just a technical one. Audiences, regulators, and distribution partners are beginning to ask harder questions about provenance.

Forward-looking organisations are treating integrity as foundational through three mechanisms:

  • Digital watermarking embedded at ingest and preserved through the delivery chain
  • Provenance frameworks built on open standards such as C2PA protocols
  • Hardware-backed authentication from verified capture and production sources

Getting this right from the start is significantly easier than retrofitting it into an existing AI-enabled workflow.

Where to Begin

For organisations mapping their AI roadmap, the most effective starting point is rarely the most ambitious one. The recommended approach: select high-friction areas where success metrics are already clear, conduct workflow audits before deploying tooling, implement governance structures early, and design integrity requirements into systems from inception rather than as an afterthought.

The Near Future

The next wave will see agentic systems taking on more decisions independently within defined boundaries. This is not an incremental improvement on current automation — it represents a fundamental shift in how operations are structured. Teams that have already built the data infrastructure, governance frameworks, and trust mechanisms will be best positioned to move quickly when these capabilities mature.

The question for media and entertainment organisations today is not whether to adopt AI as an operating layer, but how to build the foundations that will make that transition durable.
